Your modal has `z-index: 9999`. The sticky header has `z-index: 10`. The modal
renders behind the header. Someone bumps it to `999999` and nothing changes.

This is the most reliably confusing bug in CSS layout, and it is confusing for a
precise reason: **the two numbers are never compared to each other.**

## z-index is a local comparison

A z-index does not place an element on a global stack. It orders an element among
its *siblings inside one stacking context*. If two elements live in different
contexts, their z-index values never meet — what gets compared instead is the
position of their respective contexts.

So the question is never "which number is bigger". It is "do these two elements
share a stacking context, and if not, which contexts are actually competing?"

In the failing case the modal is inside a card, the card creates a stacking context,
and the modal's 9999 orders it against its siblings *inside that card*. What competes
with the header is the card itself — and the card has no z-index at all, so it sits
in document order and loses. The 9999 is not too small. It is answering a question
nobody asked.

## What creates a stacking context

Most people know `z-index` on a positioned element does it. The list is longer, and
the surprising members are the ones that bite:

- `transform` — including `translateZ(0)` and `translate3d(0,0,0)`
- `filter`, `backdrop-filter`
- `opacity` less than 1
- `will-change` naming any of the above
- `isolation: isolate`
- `contain: paint`, and being in the top layer

Note what is *not* on the list: `position: relative` on its own. A positioned element
with no z-index creates nothing, which is why the property feels safe until the day
you add a z-index to it.

The dangerous one is `transform`, because it arrives disguised as a performance hint.
Somebody added `translateZ(0)` to a card two years ago to "force GPU acceleration" —
a folk remedy that was already outdated when they wrote it — and that one line
silently rewrote the paint order for every descendant the card will ever have.

## One property, two consequences

Here is the part that makes the bug genuinely hard: the same transform does a second,
unrelated-looking thing.

A transformed element becomes the **containing block for `position: fixed`
descendants**. So the modal's `position: fixed; inset: 0` no longer resolves against
the viewport — it resolves against the card. The backdrop that was supposed to dim
the whole screen now dims a 320-pixel box in the middle of the page.

Two symptoms, one cause, and they present so differently that teams routinely file
them as separate tickets and fix neither. If you ever see a fixed overlay that is
mysteriously the wrong size *and* mysteriously behind something, stop looking at the
overlay and go find the transformed ancestor.

## The fix, and the better fix

The narrow fix is to remove the transform, or to give the card a z-index above the
header — either restores the expected order.

The real fix is to stop competing in the stacking tree at all. A native `<dialog>`
opened with `showModal()`, or an element with the `popover` attribute, is promoted to
the **top layer**: a separate layer that sits above the entire document and outside
the stacking tree, where no ancestor can trap it and no z-index is needed. It also
brings the backdrop pseudo-element, focus containment and Escape-to-close, which are
three more things the div-based modal was going to reimplement badly.

That is what ends the z-index arms race — not a bigger number, a different layer.

## What to say when you are asked

This comes up as a debugging question, and the interviewer is listening for whether
you reason about mechanism or reach for a bigger number.

Say that z-index is scoped to a stacking context, so the first move is to find the
nearest ancestor that creates one and check what *it* competes with. Name transform,
filter and opacity as the ones people do not expect. Then mention that a transformed
ancestor also captures `position: fixed`, because connecting those two symptoms to a
single cause is the part that signals you have actually debugged this rather than
read about it. Finish on the top layer, which is the answer to "how would you build
it so this cannot happen".
