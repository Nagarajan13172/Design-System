A team ships a performance fix. The dashboard says the median dropped by 700
milliseconds. Core Web Vitals do not move at all. Nobody has made a mistake, and
nobody has been dishonest, and the score is right to ignore it.

## One point versus a distribution

A lab run — Lighthouse, WebPageTest, your laptop — measures one scripted load, on
one device, over one network, with one cache state. That measurement is real. It is
also a single point, and a single point cannot describe a population.

The field is a population. The same page that loads in 900 milliseconds on the
machine under your desk has a median of about 2.2 seconds across real sessions and a
p95 past six. The lab number is not the middle of that distribution. It is near the
bottom of it, because your laptop is near the top of the device range and your
office network is near the top of the network range.

That is the whole reason a 98 and a failing field score coexist so comfortably. They
are not in conflict; they are answers to different questions.

## p75, and why the median is a trap

Core Web Vitals are graded at the **75th percentile** of real sessions. That choice
is deliberate: it means a site is judged by an experience most of its users have or
better, rather than by its best case.

It also means the median is not the number you are being scored on, and improving
the median is not the same as improving the score.

Take a concrete fix: it saves 700ms, but only on sessions that were already under
2.5 seconds — a lazy-loading change that helps when the connection is fast enough to
have already fetched everything else, say. Run it over two thousand sessions and:

- **57% of sessions get faster.** More than half your users genuinely benefit.
- **Median: 2232ms → 1532ms.** A 700ms improvement.
- **p75: 3416ms → 3416ms.** Zero. Not "a little" — none.
- **p95: unchanged.**

Every one of those numbers is true simultaneously. The fix worked, for the people it
touched. It just never touched anyone at or above the 75th percentile, and that is
the only region the score reads.

The reverse also holds, and it is the useful half: a change that compresses the tail
moves p75 even when the median does not budge at all. Removing a render-blocking
request that only slow connections ever waited on will look like nothing in your own
browser and move the score meaningfully.

**So the question to ask about any performance change is not "how much faster" but
"faster for whom, and were they already fast?"**

## Two things about the tooling that catch people out

**Lighthouse cannot measure INP.** Not "measures it approximately" — cannot, at all.
INP is defined over the interactions a real user performs across a session, and a
scripted load performs none. A Lighthouse report showing all green is silent about
the vital most real applications actually fail. If you are reasoning about INP you
need field data, and there is no lab substitute for it.

**CrUX lags by weeks.** The Chrome UX Report grades on a rolling 28-day window, so a
fix deployed yesterday is one day out of twenty-eight in the number you are looking
at. Refreshing the dashboard the morning after a deploy shows you, overwhelmingly,
the old build. Teams routinely conclude a fix "didn't work" from a number that could
not possibly have moved yet.

This is the strongest practical argument for collecting your own RUM even when CrUX
is available: your own data has no window, so you can attribute a change to a deploy
while the deploy is still fresh in everyone's mind.

## What to say when you are asked

Performance questions in interviews are diagnostic questions wearing a fix-list
costume. Asked "LCP is 4.2 seconds, what do you do?", the weak answer lists
remedies — compress images, lazy load, code split, add a CDN. The strong answer asks
which part is large, and at which percentile, and from which data source.

Then say the thing that ends the topic: that you steer on p75 field data rather than
a lab score, that you check whether a proposed change reaches the sessions that are
actually slow before spending a sprint on it, and that you do not read CrUX for a
verdict on a deploy from this week.
